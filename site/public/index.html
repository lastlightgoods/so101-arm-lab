How it is built

What runs where

The Jetson runs the arm and the cameras and records the datasets. The Mac runs the wall panel, the MLflow server and the model registry, and ships code and models to the Jetson. Training runs on Hugging Face Jobs or on the Jetson, never on the Mac.

Arm

  • SO-ARM101, leader and follower · prebuilt
  • Six Feetech STS3215 per arm · firmware 3.15, 1 Mbaud
  • Waveshare bus boards · follower 12 V
  • LeRobot 0.6.1 on both machines

Cameras

  • Top: Logitech C920 · 640×360 MJPG, 30 fps, 16.5 in up
  • Front: Logitech C920 · 42° tilt from the far side
  • Wrist: icSpring UVC on the gripper · 640×480, 22 fps by firmware
  • Settings captured per session, then frozen

Bench

  • Fiskars 18×12 in cutting mat · printed inch grid
  • Four 2 in ArUco markers · DICT_4X4_50
  • Jetson Orin Nano, tc-nano · 8 GB, 25 W
  • Mac, M1 Max · wall panel, MLflow, registry, deploys

Platform

The platform around the arm

The arm is the fun part. Most of the work is the plumbing that lets me trust what it did. Every attempt is a tracked run. Every model and every file on the Jetson has a version I can roll back to. Every change goes through tests that never touch the hardware, and nothing runs on its own without guards. This is where that stood on 16 Nov.

Data

  • Scripted closed-loop picks record their own episodes · LeRobot v3, kept and rejected tries in separate datasets
  • Each dataset gets a content hash and its own MLflow run
  • First recorded toy-to-ring episode, 11 Nov · 454 frames, three cameras

Tracking

  • Every pick and every session cycle is an MLflow run · stages, servo telemetry, judge frames
  • Model registry with aliases · candidate, jetson
  • Postgres + S3-compatible container stack; AWS mirror in Terraform · written, never applied

Training

  • Hugging Face Jobs or the Jetson, never the Mac · the cost is printed before a job starts
  • Object detector, YOLO11n, on HF Jobs · 2 runs on a T4, about $0.13
  • Record → train → register → deploy → evaluate as one resumable routine · tested on fakes, first live pass next

Deploy

  • Code to the Jetson with md5 checks, backups and one-command rollback · DEPLOYED.json records the commit
  • Models checked by sha256 from registry to edge · deploy, rollback, status
  • Refuses to deploy while the arm is busy

Tests + CI

  • 1,651 tests with no robot plugged in · fake bus, fake cameras, fake world
  • GitHub Actions on every push · about 7 minutes
  • A root guard so no test can reach the real lab · added after a test moved the arm

Safety

  • Servo trips · 65 °C stop, 60 °C start gate, 2.7 A a joint, 5 A on the bus, 10 V floor, stall, error bits
  • Fails closed if telemetry goes missing · caps of 60 tries, 240 min
  • Torque off only after a confirmed park; the wall STOP writes a stop file

Field notes

Twelve notes from six weeks

The source files for each note are listed at the end of it.

Field note 01

Locking the C920's exposure

I used two Logitech C920s because they're cheap and common. I wanted to freeze their exposure so the arm sweeping through the frame would stop changing the picture. The first attempt came back pure white.

The revision I have (USB id 046d:08e5) does three things that a plain v4l2-ctl script does not account for. A manual control is flagged inactive while its auto flag is on, so the write fails with EACCES unless the auto flag goes first. About one second after auto_exposure goes from 3 to 1 the camera forces gain to 255 on its own, overwriting whatever was written in between; that was the white frame. And at every stream start in Manual Mode it re-derives the exposure. I watched 78 come up as 156, then 312.

The lock ended up as three layers. cam_lock.py capture lets auto settle for 4 s, reads what it chose, locks, waits 1.5 s for the gain reset to pass, then tunes gain until the locked picture matches auto's mean grey within 8 levels. A resident guard checks every second and re-applies within about a second of any drift, because LeRobot opens the cameras itself when a recording starts. A udev rule re-applies 3 s after a camera re-enumerates with factory defaults. so101-record refuses to start unless check --strict passes.

On 9 Nov at 23:06 the top camera locked at exposure 38 and gain 42 where auto had chosen 83 and 109, and the picture's mean grey stayed at 112.6 with the arm in and out of frame.

Field note 02

Calibrating the table with four ArUco markers

The work surface is a Fiskars 18x12 in self-healing cutting mat. I chose it because it has an inch grid printed on it and costs very little. I glued four 2 in ArUco markers from DICT_4X4_50 onto it at grid positions (16,10), (2,10), (15,4) and (3,4), with the origin at the mat corner nearest the arm and X running along the 18 in edge.

cam_calib.py table finds the four markers in one overhead frame, snaps each to an exact square, and fits a homography from 16 corner points to mat millimetres. That is all the table calibration involves. The current solve, from 9 Nov at 23:06, puts the worst marker 0.19 mm from where the layout says it is. Under 2 mm is normal; over 5 mm usually means a marker position was measured wrong.

A homography only knows the mat surface, so height matters. locate takes a pixel and a height above the mat; a 60 mm tall box read as if it were flat lands centimetres off. I also pinned each camera's field of view from a tape-measured lens height, 16.5 in for the top camera (69.16 degrees) and 18.375 in for the oblique front camera (68.88 degrees), and treat those numbers as belonging to the lens rather than the mount.

None of the learned policies use any of this. ACT and SmolVLA look at pixels. The calibration is for the scripted moves, for the map on the wall, and for being able to say where things are in inches.

docs/camera-calibration.md, jetson/calib/top_layout.json, jetson/calib/top_table.json

Field note 03

Finding the arm's base on the mat

Knowing where the mat is in the camera does not say where the arm is on the mat. For that I switched torque off, pushed the closed jaw tip onto known grid points, and recorded the joints each time. Forward kinematics from the URDF, which match the dashboard's 3D arm to 0.1 mm, turn those joints into tip positions, and a rigid fit gives the base pose.

The first rigid fits left 15 to 30 mm of residual, far too much. The reason is a LeRobot convention: it zeroes each joint at the middle of its calibrated range, which is not exactly the URDF zero. A few degrees of offset in the lift, elbow and wrist make the kinematics read short near the base and right far out. So the fit now solves the base pose, the table height and three joint offsets together. On the real arm the offsets came out at shoulder_lift -3.9 degrees, elbow_flex -5.2 degrees and wrist_flex -0.7 degrees, and the residuals fell to 0.7 to 1.1 mm on the four points of the current fit, with an RMS of 0.69 mm. The base sits at (9.04, -3.18) in on the mat frame, heading 90.15 degrees.

One more rule came out of the jogging. The SO-101 gripper has one fixed jaw that is off the roll axis, so the contact point moves when the wrist rolls. I jogged everything at a wrist roll of 0.46 degrees and now command near that value; /api/ik defaults to it.

Field note 04

A live map of what's on the mat

The wall dashboard draws a top-down map of whatever is lying on the mat. scene_detect.py warps each overhead frame into mat space at 0.5 px per mm (229 x 152 pixels, 2 mm per pixel), subtracts a stored picture of the empty mat, and reports every blob between 40 mm² and a third of the mat with its centroid in inches, its size and its mean colour. It takes 5 to 8 ms a frame and runs twice a second.

Most of the false detections came from the arm itself. My first rule said any blob touching the arm-side edge is the arm. On 9 Nov I watched the arm reach across the mat and saw the gripper appear on the map as an object, because the forearm had left the edge through the air. The fix masks the arm from the joint readings rather than from the image: the follower's joints go through the forward kinematics, each joint origin gets a radius (30 to 50 mm, times 1.3 for the shadow), and the points are projected through the camera pose as 3D points. The projection is needed because an elbow 200 mm up under a camera 419 mm up is seen more than 100 mm away from the spot beneath it.

The other problem was flicker. A track now has to be seen in 3 consecutive detections, about 1.5 s, before it is listed, and it survives 3 missed ones, so an object the gripper passes over keeps its id.

When I fetched the map while writing this, it showed one object, id 514, at (11.26, 6.33) in, 10 by 6 mm. That is the hex nut.

docs/mat-scene.md, dashboard/scene_detect.py

Field note 05

Re-calibrating when the camera gets bumped

The overhead camera hangs on a stand the arm can reach, and the mat is only taped down. On the evening of 9 Nov the mat moved 13 to 19 mm between two frames half an hour apart, and the camera has been nudged more than once. So the calibration has to notice when it goes stale.

The four markers are detected in every frame, 2.6 ms each. When they land more than 3 mm from where the stored homography puts them, and at least 3 are visible, a fresh homography is fitted and used from then on. The server compares the stored and live solves; once the largest marker shift passes 3 px, /api/calib serves the live solve, the overlays and the 3D stage follow within a second, one event line is written ("overhead camera moved 11 px, re-solved from 4 markers (0.2 mm)"), and the file is rewritten at most once a minute, keeping the arm fit because the arm did not move when the camera did.

The camera poses are re-solved the same way, with one thing held fixed: the field of view belongs to the lens and was pinned once from a tape measure, so only position and orientation are re-solved, with a 5 mm / 1 degree hysteresis so noise does not jitter the frustums on the wall.

Limits: fewer than 3 markers visible means the last good solve is held. A mat moved more than 150 mm or 30 degrees, or markers re-glued, means calib_ok goes false and the chip on the feed reads CHECK MAT.

docs/mat-scene.md "Self-healing calibration", docs/camera-calibration.md section 6

Field note 06

Why the nut lands off target

With a calibrated camera and a fitted arm I wrote the obvious script: find the hex nut in the overhead frame, plan nine waypoints through /api/ik, pick it up, carry it to a target, set it down, measure where it landed, repeat and learn.

Two kinds of learning are in the loop. Servo sag under load, which I measured at 1 to 3.5 degrees, is corrected per waypoint from last time's error with a gain of 0.7. I tried summing the errors first and overshot by 24 mm, so only the last error is fed forward. The landing error, measured by the camera, shifts the place target with a gain of 0.5.

The sag learning worked. Over the first four runs on 10 Nov the hover error fell from 3.17 to 0.97 degrees and the lift error from 4.58 to 0.97. All four picks succeeded, each run took about 18 s, and the arm reaches within about 3 mm of a commanded point.

The nut still landed 31, 10, 39 and 21 mm from the target. The reason is mechanical: the SO-101 gripper has one moving jaw, and a 10 mm nut shifts inside it during the grasp, so the part is no longer where the tip is. The next step is to look at the part in the jaws with the wrist camera before placing, which is why that camera now has a solved hand-eye pose (1.2, 67.9, -11.3) mm from the gripper link, good to a few millimetres and about 1.5 degrees.

Field note 07

Testing without the robot

Most evenings the arm is busy, or the Jetson is training, or the cameras are being re-aimed. The code still has to be checked, so the test suite does not touch any of it. python -m pytest tests -q collected 600 tests when I first wrote this, about 3 minutes with nothing plugged in. By 16 Nov it collects 1,651, and GitHub Actions runs the whole suite on every push in about 7 minutes.

The calibration tests render checkerboards and ArUco tables through a synthetic camera with known intrinsics, distortion and pose, then check what the tool gets back: the lens model within 1.5 px, the camera position within 6 mm, pixel-to-mat within 1.5 mm, and that the arm fit refuses a mirror image. The hand-eye solver is run on a synthetic wrist camera placed on the real kinematics and must come back within 2 mm, 0.5 degrees and 2 percent of focal length. The camera lock is driven against a fake v4l2-ctl that reproduces every behaviour I measured on the real C920, the gain reset included. The bus plugin runs on a fake Feetech bus that drops packets on cue.

The dashboard page gets the same treatment with no framework and no npm: a Python stub server answers every API route from objects a test can mutate, a small Node driver opens the page in headless Chrome, blocks every external request, freezes the page's timers, and fails any test whose page threw or logged an error. Forty-five of those run in about 35 s. The camera frames in the fixtures are from 9 Nov: an empty mat, one hex nut at (10.97, 6.94) in, and the arm raised.

tests/, tests/ui/README.md, README.md

Field note 08

Every pick is a run

I kept arguing with my own memory about which change had helped, so every pick and every session cycle now logs itself to MLflow: the plan, the waypoints, each stage with its timing, the servo telemetry along the way, the frame the judge looked at, and the verdict. The Jetson and the Mac write to the same server. The learning loop is wired the same way, so far only on fakes: an evaluation trial points to a registry version, which points to a training run, which points to a dataset hash.

It paid for itself on 10 Nov. My first ten-cycle session, first10b, landed 5 of 10, and from the wall it looked like a sloppy grasp. Reading the stage lines run by run showed three separate causes. The fixed jaw was aimed at the nut's centre, so it landed on top of the nut; it now aims 10 mm behind it. A reset that the wrist camera said had failed was never checked, so the next cycle went looking where the nut wasn't; resets are judged now. And the jaws were closing 3.4 to 7 mm above a nut about 3 mm tall, so the grasp now corrects its height from the servo readback and closes about 1 mm below the mat's surface reading.

The frame here is the check that runs after every lift. The wrist camera looks at a fixed window between the jaws and fits a ring to the nut; no ring means the pick missed, and the arm gets one retry before the cycle counts as a miss.

Field note 09

Naming what's on the mat

Once the arm could pick one hex nut, I put out a mix: hex nuts, wing nuts, spacers standing and lying, a roll of tape as the target, and some toy frogs it must never touch. The map now gives each blob a kind from its size, shape and colour, and the 3D stage draws the kinds: nuts and spacers as small boxes, the tape ring as a cylinder, and the frogs as red KEEP OUT cylinders that every route, target and reset spot has to clear.

Lying spacers need the jaws to close across them, so the wrist now rolls to the spacer's long axis. On 11 Nov it turned 62 degrees for one lying the wrong way, and the arm cleared the mat: all seven toys into the roll in eight tries.

The rules are brittle where things touch or sit on the dark squares, so I tried a learned detector. A zero-shot model, OWLv2, found the frogs and the ring but was weak on nuts. A fine-tuned YOLO11n, trained twice on Hugging Face Jobs for about 13 cents, found 82% of hex nuts, 19% of wing nuts, 58% of spacers and 100% of the rings on 20 held-out frames. My bar is 95% for every class, so it is not switched on. The data is the problem: 304 frames, 70% of them one layout. The fix is a ten-minute capture session with the mat rearranged about forty times.

tools/scene_labels.py, tools/keepout.py, dashboard/scene_detect.py, the mat-detector training script, docs/mat-scene.md

Field note 10

Guards before anything runs alone

I want the arm to practise overnight. Before it does, it has to stop itself. The servos report their temperature, load, current, voltage and error bits on the same bus they are driven on, and until 11 Nov the lab read only position. Now all of it is on the wall, in MLflow, and wired to trips: 65 °C stops a session and 60 °C won't let one start, from measured idle temperatures of 52 to 57 °C. There are also current limits per joint and for the bus, a supply-voltage floor, a stall timer, and caps on tries and minutes. If the telemetry feed goes quiet, the session stops rather than guessing. Torque comes off only after a park the servos confirm, and the wall STOP writes a stop file that every loop checks.

The scariest bug so far wasn't on the arm. On 11 Nov a test in my own suite drove the real arm through a full recorded pick and moved the tape roll about 2.7 inches. A feature had landed underneath it, and the test's old assumption turned a dry run into a live one over SSH. Now a root guard swaps SSH, SCP and remote rsync in every test for a command that fails like an unreachable host, and the live dashboard answers as down. Every attempt is logged.

None of this has run unattended yet. The first supervised session with the guards on is the real test of stall loads, heat over time, and torque-off at rest.

Field note 11

Shipping code and models to the edge

For a while I copied files to the Jetson by hand, and fixes that were already on main sat undeployed: one had never reached it, another file was missing outright. Now jetson_deploy.py does it. It works out which files changed and which running programs import them, refuses if the arm is busy, backs up what it replaces, copies, checks every file's md5, restarts only what needs restarting, and writes the commit into a DEPLOYED.json on the Jetson. status compares the Jetson with main file by file, and rollback puts the last backup back.

The night it first ran, 11 Nov, it deployed twice: 9 files, then 2 more once the overnight guards had landed and the full suite had passed on that exact commit. The status afterwards was 37 files matching, 0 different and 0 missing. In review it also caught two of its own bugs: its restart would have killed its own shell, and it missed imports wrapped in try:.

Models go through the same kind of path. A checkpoint is registered in MLflow with the alias candidate, deployed to a versioned folder on the Jetson with a current link and a sha256 check from end to end, then given the alias jetson. so101-policy status exits non-zero if the registry and the Jetson disagree, for example when someone moves an alias by hand.

tools/jetson_deploy.py, tools/so101_policy.py, docs/jetson-deploy.md, docs/mlops/registry-and-deploy.md

Field note 12

Running the lab with a few agents

A lot of the code here was written by Claude agents working from my notes, and the lab has grown past one agent at a time. Now there are several: a Coordinator in Claude Code that owns the arm lab and watches the others, a laser agent for the CO2 laser jobs, a 3D-print agent that designs and prints parts on a Bambu P2S, and a purchasing agent. I write requests as numbered notes in a shared folder. Each agent works its own folder, renames a note DONE or BLOCKED when it finishes, and keeps a status document current.

A small poller runs every 5 minutes with no model calls. It reads each agent's heartbeat and flags any agent that leaves a request unread for 30 minutes. It also merges every agent's experiment entries into one shared log and writes a board summary. Decisions come to me in one place: a review panel where I approve or deny a print or a laser file next to a 3D view of the STL with its dimensions, and record results against each experiment. A click there only records a decision; the agent does the work. Anything that leaves the building waits for me at the terminal: every git push, every permissions change, every order.

The wall panel had to change too. On 14 Nov the Chrome the panel ran in hung, taking the agents' browser with it, and the Mac had to be rebooted. The panel now runs in its own native macOS window, full screen on the vertical monitor, sharing nothing with any browser. Cmd-2 switches it to the review panel.

dashboard/mac/PanelApp.swift, docs/mac-panel.md; the coordinator, poller and review panel live in a separate private repo

Status

Where the pick loop stands

The first autonomy milestone I set is ten pick-and-place cycles in a row with me at the STOP control: at least 9 of 10 landing within 15 mm of the target, and no guardrail trips. It isn't met yet. The first session, first10b on 10 Nov, landed 5 of 10 with a median error of 5.4 mm, and the guardrails ended it after three misses in a row, as designed. Note 08 has what the logs showed.

With those fixes in, an evening session on 11 Nov put 24 of 29 tries into a roll of tape used as the target. That's 83% by the overhead map's count, which probably reads one or two high because the map can mistake pieces of the roll's wall for toys. The same evening the arm cleared the mat: hex nuts, wing nuts and spacers, standing or lying, seven toys into the roll in eight tries.

Spacers rolled away when a late correction slid the open jaws across the mat, so corrections over 1.5 mm now lift, move over and come straight down: 2 of 2 since. The arm can also pick up the tape roll and move it to reset the task, lifting it with its bottom 83 mm in the air, but it sets it down 13 to 29 mm from where it was told, and I don't know why yet.
